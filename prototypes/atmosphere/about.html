About the sky prediction

Local Sunset combines the Sun’s position, your terrain horizon and a simplified atmosphere to help you plan a sunrise or sunset. The sky view is a simulation; the color peak is an experimental planning estimate.

Sunrise, sunset and your horizon

Astronomy Engine calculates the Sun’s position for the location, date and observer elevation. “Astronomical sunset” and “Astronomical sunrise” mean conventional rise/set at an unobstructed horizon, including standard refraction and the Sun’s apparent disk. They do not mean astronomical twilight, which occurs with the Sun 18° below the horizon.

The terrain calculation traces outward across a digital elevation model to find the skyline in each direction. It accounts for Earth curvature and assumed atmospheric refraction, then compares the apparent solar disk with that skyline. Mountains can hide the Sun earlier in the evening or delay it in the morning. Ground elevation plus your height above ground sets the observer’s elevation. A manual ground override and nearby DEM exclusion can help where the map does not represent your viewing point well.

Turning terrain off avoids surrounding-terrain downloads and hides local terrain event predictions. Ground elevation is still required: it comes from a matching cached terrain result, a point elevation lookup, or your manual override. Bare-earth terrain does not include individual buildings and trees. Decorative trees are visual only.

Why the sky changes color

Near the horizon, sunlight travels through more atmosphere. Molecules scatter short blue wavelengths more strongly than red ones (Rayleigh scattering), leaving warmer direct light. Aerosols add haze and scatter strongly toward the Sun. As the Sun drops, Earth’s shadow removes direct illumination from progressively higher parts of the atmosphere.

The renderer integrates single-scattered sunlight through a spherical atmosphere, including extinction along both the incoming sunlight path and the viewing path (Beer–Lambert attenuation). Molecular and aerosol densities decrease exponentially with height. Rayleigh and Henyey–Greenstein phase functions describe their angular scattering. Three representative wavelengths—680, 550 and 440 nm—approximate red, green and blue. Exposure and a display transfer curve turn the calculated radiance into screen colors.

Haze (AOD, aerosol optical depth) controls the assumed aerosol amount. It is a manual model parameter, not a live smoke or air-quality measurement. The basic atmosphere works without weather downloads. Turning Atmospheric sky off selects the earlier sky renderer; the color graph still uses the atmospheric model.

The graph, color peak and viewing window

The graph samples once per minute for up to an hour on either side of the astronomical event, within the available timeline. At each time it averages three sky directions: toward the Sun and 20° to either side. Samples are taken at least 6° above the horizontal and at least 2° above the terrain skyline. Terrain can therefore change the curve by changing which sky elevations are sampled, as well as by changing observer elevation.

  • Brightness uses relative luminance from the simulated light. It is not calibrated illuminance in lux.
  • Color potential combines relative RGB color contrast with a brightness weight, so a colorful but nearly dark sky scores less strongly. Specifically, contrast is (brightest channel − dimmest channel) / brightest channel; the score is 100 × contrast × (1 − exp(−35 × luminance)). It is an arbitrary index, not a probability of beauty.
  • Colorfulness, when shown, is the contrast term alone. Warm-color strength measures red above blue, weighted by brightness.

The modeled peak is the highest color-potential sample, provided it reaches the minimum score of 12. The recommended window is peak ± your chosen minutes (10 by default), clipped to the sampled timeline. It is a planning buffer, not a confidence interval. A peak at the graph’s boundary may lie beyond the sampled range. The peak and window are not forced to follow terrain sunset: colorful light can occur while the Sun is still visible.

Blue marks astronomical rise/set, gray marks terrain contact, green marks color peak, and white marks the selected time. The shaded area is the recommended window. Times use the location’s time zone. Realtime follows the wall clock; preview mode shows milestones relative to the simulated time.

Optional local weather

Weather forecasts are enabled by default; a saved choice to disable them is respected. The local cloud estimate downloads Open-Meteo forecasts for the selected coordinates: total and low/middle/high cloud cover, air temperature at 2 m, and wind at 10 m above ground. The app requests seven days of hourly values, interpolates them to the displayed time, and interpolates wind direction across north correctly. Wind direction is where the wind comes from. Cloud layers overlap; their percentages should not be added.

Cloud fractions drive illustrative patterns at assumed heights of 2, 5 and 10 km, with approximate twilight lighting. They are not a satellite image or the measured positions of individual clouds. Temperature and wind are displayed for planning; they do not currently alter scattering or refraction. Unavailable or out-of-range forecasts are labeled unavailable.

The weather preference and latest forecast are remembered in that browser. While enabled and the page is active, forecasts refresh about every 30 minutes and when the location or required time coverage changes. Failures retry after five minutes; existing usable data is retained. Returning to the page checks freshness. Disabling weather stops those downloads. Coordinates are sent to Open-Meteo for forecasts and point elevation lookup.

What is approximate

The model has not been calibrated against a measured sunset-beauty dataset. It omits multiple scattering, ozone absorption, full spectral color matching, refracted atmospheric ray paths and mountain shadows within the atmosphere. Deep twilight and exact display colors can differ substantially from reality. No satellite imagery, radar, spatial cloud forecast, live smoke, particulate or air-quality data is currently ingested. Local cloud fractions cannot tell whether a distant horizon gap will let sunlight through.

Weather grids, terrain resolution, viewing-point accuracy and actual refraction limit timing accuracy. Displayed seconds reflect the calculation, not guaranteed observational precision. The browser uses a small internally rendered image enlarged to the sky window to keep the simulation practical on phones.

About the sky prediction

Local Sunset combines the Sun’s position, your terrain horizon and a simplified atmosphere to help you plan a sunrise or sunset. The sky view is a simulation; the color peak is an experimental planning estimate.

Applying a location

Apply location beside the place entry looks up a new address and updates the sky and predictions. A unique result is applied directly; when several places match, choose the intended result to apply it. For an already selected or saved place, or manually edited coordinates/settings, Apply recalculates without repeating address lookup. Enter also applies. The same action replaces the former Trace button.

Apply reuses completed calculations, matching cached terrain horizons and elevation tiles where available. New coordinates or terrain settings may require recomputing the horizon and downloading missing tiles, but Apply does not clear the terrain cache or force a complete download. Terrain disabled uses point ground elevation without a surrounding-terrain trace. Applying a location does not modify saved place presets.

Clearing terrain downloads

Open Terrain settings → Terrain cache and choose Clear terrain cache. Before deletion, a confirmation shows the scope and approximate MB to be freed. The estimate counts terrain payloads, not database or filesystem overhead. It covers elevation tiles, lighting meshes, traced horizons, derived calculations and persisted terrain scene data. Browser-hosted pages clear this browser’s cache; device/server editions can also clear the server cache using the scope selector.

Clearing is blocked while terrain acquisition or calculation is running. The current image stays in memory, but its terrain payload is removed from persistent storage and is not immediately written back. Saved places, selected-location metadata, view preferences, projector settings, weather and satellite data remain. Apply location, or reload when terrain is needed, rebuilds the missing data. Cancel changes nothing.

Saved places

The saved-place dropdown below the entry selects coordinates/settings without applying them; use Apply location when ready. Save as… saves the current selection as a separate named place. Manage… opens a draft editor for names, manual coordinates, height above ground, optional ground elevation overrides and DEM exclusion distance. Add or delete places and change their order with Move up/down. Save changes commits the complete edited list; Cancel leaves it unchanged. Names must be unique. Existing location metadata is preserved.

Saving checks that the stored list still matches the one opened for editing. If another tab or device changed it, the save is rejected rather than overwriting those edits. Browser-hosted places remain in that browser’s storage; the Pi stores its list on the device. These edits do not clear terrain caches or projector settings. Selecting or managing places does not change the displayed scene until Apply.

Sunrise, sunset and your horizon

Astronomy Engine calculates the Sun’s position for the location, date and observer elevation. “Astronomical sunset” and “Astronomical sunrise” mean conventional rise/set at an unobstructed horizon, including standard refraction and the Sun’s apparent disk. They do not mean astronomical twilight, which occurs with the Sun 18° below the horizon.

The terrain calculation traces outward across a digital elevation model to find the skyline in each direction. It accounts for Earth curvature and assumed atmospheric refraction, then compares the apparent solar disk with that skyline. Mountains can hide the Sun earlier in the evening or delay it in the morning. Ground elevation plus your height above ground sets the observer’s elevation. A manual ground override and nearby DEM exclusion can help where the map does not represent your viewing point well.

Turning terrain off avoids surrounding-terrain downloads and hides local terrain event predictions. Ground elevation is still required: it comes from a matching cached terrain result, a point elevation lookup, or your manual override. Bare-earth terrain does not include individual buildings and trees. Decorative trees are visual only.

Why the sky changes color

Near the horizon, sunlight travels through more atmosphere. Molecules scatter short blue wavelengths more strongly than red ones (Rayleigh scattering), leaving warmer direct light. Aerosols add haze and scatter strongly toward the Sun. As the Sun drops, Earth’s shadow removes direct illumination from progressively higher parts of the atmosphere.

The renderer integrates single-scattered sunlight through a spherical atmosphere, including extinction along both the incoming sunlight path and the viewing path (Beer–Lambert attenuation). Molecular and aerosol densities decrease exponentially with height. Rayleigh and Henyey–Greenstein phase functions describe their angular scattering. The fallback RGB atmosphere uses three representative wavelengths—680, 550 and 440 nm. The spectral mode described below samples more wavelengths. Exposure and a display transfer curve turn the calculated radiance into screen colors.

Haze (AOD, aerosol optical depth) controls the assumed aerosol amount. It is a manual model parameter, not a live smoke or air-quality measurement. The basic atmosphere works without weather downloads. Turning Atmospheric sky off selects the earlier sky renderer; the color graph still uses the atmospheric model.

Spectral twilight and cloud color

Spectral mode samples 16 bands from 400–700 nm and converts spectra using analytic CIE matching approximations and a linear sRGB transform. It assumes a 5778 K solar spectrum. A broad Chappuis-band absorption approximation and a default 300 DU ozone column model ozone’s contribution to twilight color. Ozone is assumed, not measured. Warm direct light mixed with bluer diffuse sky illumination can produce pink, purple and other twilight colors; the renderer estimates these contributions rather than applying a fixed purple filter.

Cloud illumination combines attenuated direct sunlight with diffuse light from four sampled sky directions. Full atmospheric multiple scattering, detailed cloud interiors and refracted atmospheric ray paths are not modeled. Spectral effects change the image; the graph and peak timing retain the original RGB model.

Sunlight on mountains and the moving sunline

GPU 3D terrain is a separate rendering mode using WebGPU where available, with WebGL 2 and Standard fallbacks. New interactive sessions start in GPU mode; saved renderer choices are respected, and unattended projector sessions retain Standard. Quality and diagnostic controls are inside the folded GPU settings section.

GPU sunlight is calculated spectrally at terrain-vertex elevation, including the regional change of local solar altitude. A smoothly interpolated lookup table avoids elevation bands. The direct-light display response increases monotonically with irradiance and compresses bright daylight; it is a visual exposure calibration, not a lux measurement. Diffuse fill is derived from a cosine-weighted integration of the modeled sky hemisphere, with approximate ground reflection. This lets exposed peaks receive bright warm light while shaded slopes retain sky-colored illumination.

GPU mountain shadows use a directional Sun and a focused shadow map covering nearby receivers within roughly 8 km, while retaining the wider 60 km mesh as blockers. Shadow resolution, bias and DEM sampling limit the sunline accuracy. The optional finer patch uses a 50 m mesh within 6.4 km, with source resolution varying by location. The GPU sky currently remains a clear-sky approximation: satellite cloud rendering and cloud attenuation remain in Standard. Neither mode reconstructs measured snow, trees or buildings.

Optional terrain sunlight uses a cached 60 km-wide bare-earth mesh. Grid spacing is 125 m within 8 km of the observer along each mesh axis, and about 690 m outside that central area; underlying elevation-data resolution can be coarser. A background worker computes each surface point’s horizon toward the Sun, including Earth curvature and the change of solar altitude across the region. A high western ridge can receive eastern sunrise light while the valley—and your viewing location—remain in shadow.

The Sun’s finite angular diameter softens the sunline. Terrain polygons are clipped across the moving illumination boundary rather than giving a whole face the average lighting of its corners. Slope normals set the cosine response to direct sunlight, and gradients smooth the shading across faces. Direct spectral transmission determines the sunlight’s color independently of cooler ambient sky light.

The surface uses an assumed subdued earth/vegetation reflectance. Incoming light is multiplied by this reflectance; shared-channel highlight compression preserves color instead of clipping the terrain toward white. Haze adds a restrained distance effect. These surface colors, exposure and haze are illustrative, not measured reflectance or calibrated photometry. Actual vegetation, rock and snow cover are not reconstructed.

With Account for clouds along sunlight path checked, coarse spatial samples use satellite cloud lookups toward the Sun; missing paths use the local forecast cloud fraction as an approximate attenuation. This is not a complete cloud-shadow forecast. Uncheck it for a clear-sun comparison of the terrain lighting only. Mountain blockers beyond the mesh, finer ridges, hidden cloud layers and volumetric light shafts remain unresolved. The original detailed skyline and numerical event times remain authoritative. Phones draw fewer triangles, and the terrain image is reused between relevant lighting or view changes.

The graph, color peak and viewing window

The graph samples once per minute for up to an hour on either side of the astronomical event, within the available timeline. At each time it averages three sky directions: toward the Sun and 20° to either side. Samples are taken at least 6° above the horizontal and at least 2° above the terrain skyline. Terrain can therefore change the curve by changing which sky elevations are sampled, as well as by changing observer elevation.

  • Brightness uses relative luminance from the simulated light. It is not calibrated illuminance in lux.
  • Color potential combines relative RGB color contrast with a brightness weight, so a colorful but nearly dark sky scores less strongly. Specifically, contrast is (brightest channel − dimmest channel) / brightest channel; the score is 100 × contrast × (1 − exp(−35 × luminance)). It is an arbitrary index, not a probability of beauty.
  • Colorfulness, when shown, is the contrast term alone. Warm-color strength measures red above blue, weighted by brightness.

The modeled peak is the highest color-potential sample, provided it reaches the minimum score of 12. The recommended window is peak ± your chosen minutes (10 by default), clipped to the sampled timeline. It is a planning buffer, not a confidence interval. A peak at the graph’s boundary may lie beyond the sampled range. The peak and window are not forced to follow terrain sunset: colorful light can occur while the Sun is still visible.

Blue marks astronomical rise/set, gray marks terrain contact, green marks color peak, and white marks the selected time. The shaded area is the recommended window. Times use the location’s time zone. Realtime follows the wall clock; preview mode shows milestones relative to the simulated time.

Optional local weather

Weather forecasts are enabled by default; a saved choice to disable them is respected. The local cloud estimate downloads Open-Meteo forecasts for the selected coordinates: total and low/middle/high cloud cover, air temperature at 2 m, wind at 10 m above ground, precipitation probability, total precipitation, rain, showers and new snowfall. The app requests seven days of hourly values. Temperature, wind and cloud fractions are interpolated to the displayed time; wind direction is interpolated across north correctly. Wind direction is where the wind comes from. Cloud layers overlap; their percentages should not be added.

Where satellite geometry is unavailable, cloud fractions drive illustrative patterns at assumed heights of 2, 5 and 10 km, with approximate twilight lighting. They are not a satellite image or the measured positions of individual clouds. Temperature and 10 m wind are displayed for planning; they do not alter scattering or refraction. Separate winds aloft from the humidity profile drive the satellite-cloud projection described below. Unavailable or out-of-range forecasts are labeled unavailable.

Precipitation is displayed for the one-hour interval containing the selected time. The provider timestamps hourly totals and probability at the end of the preceding hour, so these quantities are selected by interval rather than linearly interpolated into an instantaneous rate. Total precipitation is liquid-water equivalent in millimeters and inches; new snowfall is depth in centimeters and inches. Rain and showers are separate components and should not be added to the already inclusive total. Unavailable values are not treated as zero. These are model forecasts, not a rain gauge or confirmation of current snowfall. Scrubbing backward does not automatically retrieve archived historical weather; dates outside the loaded forecast remain unavailable.

The weather preference and latest forecast are remembered in that browser. While enabled and the page is active, forecasts refresh about every 30 minutes and when the location or required time coverage changes. Failures retry after five minutes; existing usable data is retained. Returning to the page checks freshness. Disabling weather stops those downloads. Coordinates are sent to Open-Meteo for forecasts and point elevation lookup.

Satellite clouds, bases and short-term movement

Experimental cloud reconstruction downloads NOAA GOES-19 ABI CONUS products: cloud mask (ACM), retrieved cloud-top height (ACHA), and optical depth (COD) when available. Height-based satellite parallax correction helps place the mask on the curved Earth. A 96×96 regional grid spans approximately 440 km north–south and east–west, centered on the viewing location. Missing retrievals remain unknown. The renderer checks the sightline outward so an intersected nearby cloud can obscure a distant clearing. Beyond regional coverage or unresolved geometry, the image can use forecast-pattern fallback; a clear-looking area is not necessarily an observed clearing.

Cloud tops alone do not determine thickness. An optional Open-Meteo GFS humidity/temperature profile uses 25 hPa pressure-level sampling to estimate candidate bases from water/ice near-saturation layers. Ice saturation is evaluated from temperature assuming the provider’s relative humidity is water-referenced. Ambiguous or vertically unresolved layers are rejected. This is one local column, limited to approximately 50 km and ±90 minutes; it is not a measured three-dimensional cloud base. Unsupported bases use the manually assumed cloud thickness. Missing optical depth uses an assumed optical depth of 2.

Satellite acquisition follows realtime, playback and time scrubbing. Historical requests select a cloud-mask observation at or before the requested time; cloud-top and optical-depth products are aligned nearby in time and can have different timestamps. A cached observation within ten minutes can be reused. Larger changes request matching imagery after a short debounce; an in-flight request finishes before the app catches up to a newer scrub position. Future times use the latest available observation, not a future satellite image.

Wind projection backtraces cloud positions with winds interpolated by height from the local profile. Projection is allowed for up to 90 minutes after observation; beyond 30 minutes it is labeled extended and reduced-confidence. Distant clouds outside the local wind coverage stay at their observed positions. With wind projection off, a still observation is used for up to 60 minutes after acquisition. Views before the observation or beyond the applicable limit do not apply it. This does not model cloud growth, decay, convection or measured motion from successive satellite images. Panning uses the same geographic cloud field in all directions, with coverage and height limits still applying.

The Pi preprocesses NOAA files locally. On static hosting, a background worker reads the NOAA NetCDF/HDF5 products using NIST h5wasm from jsDelivr. First use downloads roughly 6 MB of decoder plus satellite products; refreshes can download several MB. NOAA CONUS files are cropped locally rather than sending viewing coordinates to NOAA. Weather/profile requests send coordinates to Open-Meteo; the profile uses coordinates rounded to 0.01°. Disabling satellite acquisition stops its automatic downloads.

Exploring time without an endpoint

The textured Continuous time strip below the scenery is a relative control: swipe left for later and right for earlier, then lift and swipe again to continue. Choose 2 or 12 hours per full-width swipe. There is no fixed slider thumb or endpoint. Crossing midnight calculates the appropriate local date’s events while preserving the viewing direction; local dates follow the location’s time zone, including daylight-saving changes. The date-loading label identifies a pending change. Swiping enters preview mode; Go Realtime restores the current sky. The event graph slider remains focused on the sunrise/sunset window.

The strip supports mouse dragging and touch. Arrow keys move five minutes; Shift+arrows move one hour; Page Up/Down move six hours. Horizontal scrolling or Shift+wheel also moves time. Historical/future cloud and forecast limits still apply, even though the time control itself continues.

On-image forecast

The Forecast ▾ control on the image expands precipitation, temperature, wind and cloud-cover information for the selected time. It starts collapsed and remembers your choice across reloads, including projector view. Collapsing hides the readout without disabling weather downloads; the separate Enable weather forecast checkbox controls acquisition. The current time and next-event countdown remain visible.

About this image and loading status

About this image, beside the scenery controls, reports selected time separately from the last rendered frame. It lists pending downloads/rendering, satellite mask/top observation times, profile time, projection offset, assumed thickness, forecast download time, active atmosphere and terrain effects, and fallback status. Cloud-source percentages count sampled sky-buffer pixels using satellite lookup, forecast fallback or no cloud data; they are not cloud-cover percentages.

While matching cloud data loads, the display may briefly retain a nearby previous frame or render a temporary atmosphere-only background. Loading badges and the dialog identify these states. A temporary clear atmosphere is not a claim that the real sky is clear. Data failures retain usable data where applicable and retry automatically.

Weather, spectral sky, satellite acquisition, base estimation, wind projection and terrain lighting start enabled. Controls with stored preferences respect saved opt-outs. The atmospheric model can also run without network data. Browser workers, caching and reduced image/mesh detail keep the prototype practical on phones, although performance depends on the device.

What is approximate

The model has not been calibrated against a measured sunset-beauty dataset. It omits full multiple scattering, detailed cloud microphysics, refracted atmospheric ray paths and volumetric mountain shadows in the sky. Ozone absorption and spectral color matching are approximate when spectral mode is enabled. There is no radar ingestion or live smoke, particulate, aerosol-composition or air-quality assimilation. Cloud growth, precipitation particles, snow-covered surface reflectance and hidden cloud layers are not simulated. Deep twilight, exact display colors, cloud boundaries and shadow timing can differ substantially from reality.

Weather grids, terrain resolution, viewing-point accuracy and actual refraction limit timing accuracy. Displayed seconds reflect the calculation, not guaranteed observational precision. The browser uses a small internally rendered image enlarged to the sky window to keep the simulation practical on phones.
